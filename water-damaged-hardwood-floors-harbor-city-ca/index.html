Water Damaged Hardwood Floors: Dry in Place or Replace?

Wood takes up water slowly and gives it back slower still. What the boards do in the first week tells you which road you are on.

By the Harbor City Water Damage Pros team · Published 2026-10-28

What your boards are telling you

Hardwood swells across its width as it absorbs moisture, and the shape of that swelling is a clue. The table below pairs the common symptoms with their usual causes and the typical next step. Treat it as a map, not a diagnosis, since only readings on the boards and subfloor can confirm what is happening.

Likely causes and next steps are typical patterns, not diagnoses.

Cupping, edges higher than the centerMoisture from below, such as a slab, crawlspace or plumbing leak, or one wet side of the boardFind and stop the source, dry the floor and subfloor, then reassess once readings settle
Crowning, center higher than the edgesOften a cupped floor that was sanded too early, or moisture from aboveLet the floor reach equilibrium, then sand flat only after readings are stable
Buckling, boards lifting off the subfloorHeavy saturation, such as a flood or a long running leakRemove affected boards, since most buckled sections will not return to flat
Black or dark gray stainsWater reaching bare wood through the finish over time, sometimes with moldAssess for removal of boards, because sanding may not bring the color back
White haze or cloudy finishSurface moisture trapped in the finishAllow it to dry, then screen or recoat as needed
Gaps opening between boardsBoards shrinking after drying outWait for stable humidity, then fill or replace as needed
Soft or spongy feel underfootSaturated subfloor or rotOpen the floor to inspect and likely replace the subfloor section

Dry in place or replace

Clean water that was caught within a day or two gives hardwood a fair chance. The boards may cup for a week or two while they dry, then relax. Floors that sat in Category 3 water, or that buckled, are far less likely to be saved, because contamination cannot be cleaned out of the wood.

Time is the deciding factor, plus the water source. Clean supply water, caught quickly, favors drying. Storm or sewage water, or days of soaking, usually points toward replacement.

Solid versus engineered

Solid hardwood is one thickness of wood, and can often be sanded more than once. Engineered boards have a thin wear layer glued over plywood or fiber core layers. They tolerate a spill better in some ways, but they delaminate when the glue lines stay wet, and a thin wear layer limits the sanding left.

How mat drying systems work

A mat or panel system lays a perforated mat over the floor and covers it with a sheet. A vacuum pulls air down through the boards and into the room, while a dehumidifier handles the moisture that comes out. Some crews drill small holes in the toe kick or use the gaps between boards to reach the space under them.

The aim is to pull moisture out the way it went in, from the bottom up. The alternative is air blown across the top, which dries the surface and can leave the core wet. Drying one side faster than the other is how floors crack and cup in the first place.

How long it takes

Expect several days of active drying for a typical floor, and longer for thick boards, a damp slab or a second layer of flooring underneath. Typical is not a promise. Boards often keep moving for a week or two after the equipment leaves, so judge the floor after it settles.

The job is done when moisture readings in the wood match an unaffected area of the same floor, and the subfloor reads dry too. A floor that looks fine at the surface can still hold moisture in the core.

Check the subfloor and slab

Floors dry from both sides, so the layer underneath has to be checked. Plywood subfloor can be read with a pin meter. A concrete slab is trickier, since it can release moisture for weeks, and slab testing such as in-situ relative humidity probes or calcium chloride kits gives a more reliable number than a surface meter.

If the water source was a slab leak or an under-floor plumbing line, the floor will keep coming back wet until the pipe is fixed. Dry the floor, repair the source and confirm with readings before anything is installed over it.

Sand and refinish only after the readings are right

Sanding a floor that is still wet seals in moisture and often leaves it crowned. A refinisher should ask for readings first, in the wood and the subfloor, and wait until they are stable across several visits. A rushed refinish is the most common way a rescued floor fails a few months later.

Once the numbers hold, cupped but undamaged boards can often be sanded flat and refinished. Damaged sections get replaced, then blended into the field. Our water damage repair crews coordinate that rebuild after the drying done by water mitigation.

Leak sources and permits in Harbor City

Not every wet floor starts with your own plumbing. If water came from a main break in the street, the LADWP water main break line is 1-800-DIAL-DWP (1-800-342-5397), and calling it is worth doing before you start tearing out boards. Take photos of the water source and the floor first, since the cause affects who you speak to next.

If the leak was a supply line in your home, fixing it may be a plumbing job. A supply line repair is plumbing work, and LADBS handles permits for modifications to potable water piping. Ask LADBS if your plumbing repair needs a permit, and confirm that your plumber will obtain it.

Sources: LADWP, LADBS

Frequently asked questions

Can cupped hardwood go back to flat?

Often yes, if the moisture source is stopped and the floor dries slowly and evenly. It can take weeks to settle, and then a light sanding may be enough. Floors that buckled or sat in contaminated water are less likely to recover.

Should I remove the baseboards?

Usually yes. Pulling baseboards lets the crew reach the wall cavity and gives the floor room to dry and expand. We take photos before they come off and set them aside for reuse.

Can I use fans and a space heater?

Fans alone can dry the surface and trap moisture in the core, and a heater can make boards cup or split. Controlled dehumidification with the right airflow is gentler. Pick the method after readings.

Will my insurance cover floor replacement?

Sudden and accidental leaks are often covered and slow ones often are not, so check your policy or ask your insurer. We provide a written scope and moisture logs you can hand to your insurer.

Should I call LADWP if my floor flooded from a leak in the street?

If water is coming from a main in the street, yes. Yes, LADWP lists that main break line and it is worth calling before you pull boards. Then call us to begin drying, and keep your photos.
